Password and account management

Controlling who has access to what, and keeping credentials strong and safe.

What it is

Stolen or weak credentials are one of the most common ways attackers get in. This area covers how accounts are created, what permissions they get, how passwords and other secrets are stored and how access is removed.

Good practice means every person has their own account, gets only the access they need, uses multi-factor authentication and loses access as soon as they no longer need it.

The account lifecycle

  1. ProvisionCreate an individual account when someone joins.
  2. GrantGive only the permissions the role requires.
  3. ProtectRequire strong passwords and multi-factor authentication, and store shared secrets in a vault.
  4. ReviewRegularly check who has access and remove what isn't needed.
  5. DeprovisionDisable accounts promptly when someone leaves.

In practice

What it looks like in a real environment

Typical scenarios from organizations across the industry.

Example

Someone leaves the team

Their accounts are disabled the same day, instead of lingering for months as an easy way in.

Example

Retiring the password spreadsheet

Shared admin passwords move out of a spreadsheet into a password vault that controls who can see each one and logs every access.

Example

MFA saves the day

An employee's password leaks in another website's breach. An attacker tries it, but the sign-in is blocked because it requires a second factor they don't have.

Key terms

Least privilege
Giving each account only the access it needs.
MFA
Multi-factor authentication: proving identity with more than just a password.
Deprovisioning
Removing access when it's no longer needed.
Privileged account
An account with admin rights, which needs extra protection.
